# Rule 30's block algebra is never a quasigroup and its multiplication monoid is solvable for every block size, like those of Rule 90 and of the P-complete Rule 110, so the algebraic theory of fast prediction neither applies nor separates

**Question.** A classical line of work asks which cellular automata can be predicted faster than by simulation from
the algebra of their local rule. Moore ([Quasi-linear cellular automata](https://arxiv.org/abs/adap-org/9701001),
*Physica D* 103, 1997; [Predicting non-linear cellular automata quickly by decomposing them into linear
ones](https://arxiv.org/abs/patt-sol/9701008), *Physica D* 111, 1998) writes a rule on a staggered lattice as a binary
operation $a * b$ and shows that when it is a quasigroup or group with enough structure (linear over an abelian group,
and many nonlinear and nonabelian cases), $t$ steps can be predicted in parallel time polylogarithmic in $t$ or
serial time near $t$, although the rule is nonlinear (the abstracts list serial times $t$, $t \log t$, $t \log^2 t$,
$t^\alpha$ with $\alpha < 2$ and parallel times $\log t$ to $\log^2 t$). On the other side, Barrington's theorem
(*JCSS* 38, 1989) and Barrington and Thérien (*JACM* 35, 1988) put the word problem of a finite monoid in ACC$^0$ when
every subgroup is solvable and make it NC$^1$-complete when a nonsolvable group divides it. Where does Rule 30's
operation sit on this map, and does its position say anything about Problem 3?

**What was done.** For an elementary rule $f$ and a block size $k \ge 2$, a block is $a \in \{0,1\}^k$ and

$$(a * b)_m = f(w_m, w_{m+1}, w_{m+2}), \qquad w = (a_0, \dots, a_{k-1}, b_0, \dots, b_{k-1}),\ 0 \le m < k,$$

which is one step of the rule on the two blocks, written on the $k$ cells one place to the right of $a_0$ (the brief's
two-block form is $k = 2$). The right translation by $b$ is $\rho_b(a) = a * b$, the left translation by $a$ is
$\lambda_a(b) = a * b$, $G_k$ is the group generated by the bijective $\rho_b$, and $M_k$ is the transformation monoid
of $\{0,1\}^k$ generated by all $\rho_b$ and $\lambda_a$. `computations/block_algebra.py` builds the table, computes
$|G_k|$ by the Schreier-Sims algorithm, enumerates $M_k$ where it is small, and lists the group induced on the image of
each idempotent (isomorphic to the maximal subgroup there). The test checks the table against direct simulation, the
group orders against brute-force closure, the translations against the kit's states of automaton group 2369, the rank
bounds of the proof below, and the solvability test on $A_5$ and $S_4$.

## Outcome

**Theorem 1 (the units are a 2-group, and they are group 2369).** Let $f$ be left-permutive, $f(l, c, r) = l \oplus
h(c, r)$. For every $k$, each $\rho_b$ is the bijection $a_m \mapsto a_m \oplus h(w_{m+1}, w_{m+2})$, in which
coordinate $m$ is flipped by a function of coordinates above $m$ (of $a$, or of $b$, which is fixed). So $\rho_b$
lies in the group $W_k$ of such triangular maps, the iterated wreath product $C_2 \wr \dots \wr C_2$ of order
$2^{2^k - 1}$ (the automorphisms of the depth-$k$ binary tree read from coordinate $k-1$ down, a Sylow 2-subgroup of
$\mathrm{Sym}(2^k)$). Hence $G_k \le W_k$ is a 2-group: nilpotent, so solvable. For Rule 30, $\rho_b$ depends on $b$
only through $b_0$ and $b_0 \lor b_1$, so there are three of them, and under the bit reversal $y(e) = a_{k-1-e}$ they
are the kit's three states `PhiS 0`, `PhiS 1`, `PhiS 3` modulo $2^k$ (`Rule30Equivalents.AutomatonGroup`), for
$(b_0, b_1) = (0, 0), (0, 1), (1, \cdot)$. So **$G_k$ is automaton group 2369 acting on level $k$ of the binary
tree**: the left multiplication group of the brief is not a new object, and its 2-group property is the kit's
observation that the group is a tree automorphism group.

| $k$ | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $\log_2 \lvert G_k \rvert$, Rule 30 (computed) | 3 | 7 | 13 | 25 | 47 |
| $\log_2 \lvert W_k \rvert = 2^k - 1$ | 3 | 7 | 15 | 31 | 63 |

$G_k = W_k$ for $k \le 3$ (computed), and $G_k \ne W_k$ for every $k \ge 4$ (proved): $W_k$ maps onto $C_2^k$ (the
sign of each level), so it needs $k$ generators, and $G_k$ has three. Rule 45 gives the same orders; Rules 90 and 150
give $2^2, 2^4, 2^5, 2^7, 2^8$ and $2^3, 2^5, 2^6, 2^8, 2^9$ for $k = 2..6$ (the abelian translation groups of a
linear rule are small).

**Theorem 2 (the left translations have rank at most 3).** For every elementary rule and $k \ge 2$, coordinates
$m \le k - 3$ of $a * b$ do not read $b$, and coordinates $k-2$, $k-1$ read it only through $(b_0, b_1)$, so
$\lvert \lambda_a(\{0,1\}^k) \rvert \le 4$. For Rule 30, if $a_{k-1} = 1$ the top two coordinates are
$(a_{k-2} \oplus 1, 1 \oplus (b_0 \lor b_1))$, two values; if $a_{k-1} = 0$ they are $(a_{k-2} \oplus b_0, b_0 \lor b_1)$,
which takes the three values from $(b_0, b_1) = 00, 01, 1\cdot$. So $\lambda_a$ has rank at most 3, for every $k$.
In particular **the block algebra of Rule 30 is not a quasigroup for any $k$** ($3 < 2^k$), so it is not isotopic to
any group, and none of Moore's quasigroup theorems applies to it. The same holds for every elementary rule once
$k \ge 3$; at $k = 2$ the bipermutive Rules 90 and 150 are quasigroups (their $\lambda_a$ are bijections) and Rule 30
is not.

**Theorem 3 (the monoid is solvable for every $k$).** For Rule 30 and every $k \ge 2$, every subgroup of $M_k$ is a
2-group or embeds in $S_3$; so every simple group dividing $M_k$ is $C_2$ or $C_3$, and $M_k$ is solvable.

*Proof.* An element of $M_k$ is a word in the generators. If the word contains some $\lambda_a$, its image has at most
3 points, since $\lvert \mathrm{im}(s \circ t) \rvert \le \min(\lvert \mathrm{im}\, s \rvert, \lvert \mathrm{im}\, t
\rvert)$ (Theorem 2). Otherwise it lies in $G_k$, a 2-group (Theorem 1). A subgroup of a transformation monoid lies in
one group $\mathcal H$-class, with identity an idempotent $e$, and acts faithfully on $\mathrm{im}\, e$ (if $s, s'$ in
it agree on $\mathrm{im}\, e$ then $s = se = s'e = s'$), so it embeds in $\mathrm{Sym}(\mathrm{im}\, e)$. If
$e = \mathrm{id}$ the subgroup lies in the units, which are $G_k$ because a word containing a map of rank below $2^k$ is
not a bijection; otherwise $\lvert \mathrm{im}\, e \rvert \le 3$. By the Krohn-Rhodes theory every simple group
dividing a finite monoid divides one of its subgroups, and the simple divisors of 2-groups and of subgroups of $S_3$
are $C_2$ and $C_3$. $\square$

Both occur: $C_2$ in $G_k$, and $S_3$ at the rank-3 idempotents for $k = 2, 3$, where $M_2$ and $M_3$ have 180 and
7,432 elements with groups $1$, $C_2$, $S_3$ at the idempotents of rank 1, 2, 3 (computed; for $k \ge 4$ only the bound
of Theorem 3 is claimed). The same proof gives, for every left-permutive elementary rule and every $k$, subgroups that
are 2-groups or embed in $S_4$ (at $k = 2$ every subgroup lies in $S_4$ anyway): solvable. This includes the linear
Rule 90.

**Rule 110 lands in the same place (computed, $k \le 5$).** Rule 110 is permutive in no input, so no $\rho_b$ is a
bijection, and for $k \ge 3$ no $\lambda_a$ is either (Theorem 2), so its units are trivial. Its monoids $M_2, \dots, M_5$ have 236, 4,580, 50,393 and 645,120 elements; at
$k = 2, 3$ the groups at the idempotents are $1, C_2, S_3$ and, at $k = 3$, $S_4$, all solvable, and at $k = 4, 5$ no
idempotent of rank 5 or more has a nontrivial group, so every subgroup again embeds in $S_4$. Predicting Rule 110 from
an arbitrary row is P-complete (Neary and Woods, ICALP 2006, in [the literature](../../literature/complexity.md)), yet
on this range its block monoid has the same simple divisors, $C_2$ and $C_3$, as Rule 30's and Rule 90's.

## Where it stops

1. **Moore's fast algorithms need a quasigroup, and Rule 30 is one-sided.** Theorem 2 rules out the hypothesis for
   every block size. What Rule 30 has instead is one-sided: bijective right translations forming a 2-group, which is
   the left-permutivity that every entry on the right-edge frame already uses, here under the name group 2369.
2. **Solvability of the monoid does not reach the triangle.** The value of a cell after $t$ steps is a product
   tree, a term in the free magma whose $2t + 1$ leaves are the input cells, not a word in $M_k$. A word in $M_k$
   describes only a computation in which one block moves and every other argument is a known constant, for example the
   black-wall formula $c(t) = $ bit $t - 1$ of $a^t(0)$ in the kit. Barrington-Thérien bounds apply to words over a
   fixed monoid, and that formula needs level $k \approx t$, where $\lvert G_k \rvert$ is $2^{47}$ already at $k = 6$:
   no fixed $k$ suffices.
3. **The invariant does not separate.** Rule 90 (linear, predictable in logarithmic parallel time) and Rule 110
   (P-complete from arbitrary rows) have solvable block monoids with the same simple divisors as Rule 30's, for every
   $k$ (Rule 90, proved) and for $k \le 5$ (Rule 110, computed). So no upper or lower bound for Rule 30's prediction can
   come from the solvability of its block algebra, in either direction. The nonsolvable side, which would make
   word problems NC$^1$-complete, is not available to Rule 30 at any block size (Theorem 3).
4. Even a P-completeness theorem from arbitrary rows would not bound $c(n)$ from one seed
   ([the Problem 3 barrier](problem-three-barrier.md)); this route does not get that far.

**Relation to other entries.** The group $G_k$ is the level-$k$ image of the automaton group studied in
[the automaton-group sections](automaton-group-sections.md), which counts states of the transducers of powers
$\Phi^{2^k}$; the orders of the level images and the monoid with the left translations are not there. Theorem 2 is
the block form of the fact that two adjacent columns determine every column to their left, so information from the
right enters a block through two cells.

**What would change this.** An algebraic invariant of the block algebra, finer than the simple divisors of its monoid,
that differs between Rule 90 and Rule 110 and is computable for Rule 30; or a decomposition of the triangle of
products into words over a fixed monoid, which Moore obtains for quasigroups and which the rank bound of Theorem 2 does
not provide.
